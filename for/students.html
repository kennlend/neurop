What is actually going wrong when you can't focus?

You'll recognise the phrases. "I read it and nothing sticks." "I'm wired but tired." "Fine until 3 pm, then gone." "I need one more coffee to start and I'm already shaky."

Part of it is sleep. A review of sleepiness among college students found that about half report daytime sleepiness and around 70 per cent get insufficient sleep [1].

Part of it is the caffeine itself. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, the ones that build up sleepiness as the day goes on. Doses from about 40 mg to 300 mg improve alertness, vigilance and reaction time in the research, though the effects on judgement and higher-order thinking are less consistent [2]. That second half matters for revision. Caffeine helps you stay with the page. It doesn't read the page for you.

And part of it is tolerance. In a double-blind study of 369 caffeine consumers, much of the apparent boost in regular users was the reversal of withdrawal, and people who didn't drink caffeine regularly reported more jitteriness [3]. If you live on coffee in exam season, a good share of what you feel as "focus" is just getting back to normal.

Why don't energy drinks, all-nighters and extra coffee fix it?

Energy drinks. They do something. After a short night, an 80 mg caffeine energy drink cut sleepy-driving incidents in a simulator for about 90 minutes [4]. But in controlled studies caffeine was the main active ingredient and sugar added little [5]. A 250 ml can of Red Bull has 80 mg of caffeine and 27 g of sugar, according to the manufacturer's UK page [6]. A 500 ml Monster carries about 160 mg, according to label listings [7]. A WHO Europe review found the health risks of energy drinks are mainly linked to caffeine, with heavy use among young people a growing concern [8]. You pay for sugar you didn't need.

All-nighters. Caffeine can reduce the damage of sleep loss. In US Navy SEAL trainees after three days awake, 200 to 300 mg improved vigilance and reaction time [9]. That was extreme military training with a lab-tested dose, not a normal night in your halls. The review of college-student sleepiness points to sleep as the fix, with consequences of too little including lower grade point averages and compromised learning [1]. The honest position is that nothing in a pouch replaces a night's sleep.

More coffee. Coffee is a perfectly good drink, and its caffeine is hard to count. A lab analysis of 97 shop espressos found 25 mg to 214 mg per cup [10]. A US study found a 16 oz brewed coffee averaged 188 mg, and the same drink from the same shop swung between 259 mg and 564 mg on different days [11]. In a survey of 1,248 US college students, 92 per cent used caffeine, averaging about 173 mg a day among users, mostly from coffee [12]. That's a US sample, and we found no equivalent for European students. Still, if you drink coffee through a revision session you may be well past a number you thought you were at.

EFSA considers up to 400 mg of caffeine a day from all sources, and single doses up to 200 mg, unlikely to raise safety concerns for healthy adults [13]. Two US-sized brewed coffees (about 188 mg each) and a 500 ml Monster (about 160 mg) come to roughly 536 mg.

Caffeine plus L-theanine: the attention pairing

This is the heart of the formula. Each pouch has 100 mg caffeine and 150 mg L-theanine, a ratio of 1.5 to 1.

Studies on the caffeine and L-theanine pairing found better attention switching and, in some trials, higher alertness:

  • In 44 young adults, 97 mg theanine with 40 mg caffeine improved accuracy when switching between tasks and raised self-reported alertness [21].
  • In 27 healthy volunteers, 100 mg theanine plus 50 mg caffeine improved the speed and accuracy of task switching compared with placebo [22].
  • A 2014 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials found moderate benefits on alertness and attention switching within two hours of a dose [14].

Task switching is relevant to a student. Moving from your notes to a past paper to a half-written essay is task switching.

The limits are real. These were acute, single-dose studies in healthy young adults, and some were funded by Unilever. Another trial at the same low doses found task switching improved but self-rated alertness did not [23]. Adding theanine made no difference over either ingredient alone in a two-hour vigilance task [24]. And at a high caffeine dose of 250 mg, theanine did not significantly reduce jitteriness, although it blunted caffeine's rise in blood pressure [15]. No trial has tested 150 mg theanine with 100 mg caffeine. We sit inside the ratios studied, which ran from about 0.8:1 to 2.4:1, but we haven't been trialled ourselves. Our page on L-theanine and caffeine goes through all of this in detail.

Rhodiola rosea: the exam-period study, told straight

The one Rhodiola trial built around students is small, and we'll describe it as it is. In a small double-blind pilot, foreign students at a Russian institution took a standardised Rhodiola extract for 20 days during an exam period. They scored better than the placebo group on physical fitness, mental fatigue and neuro-motor tests (p < 0.01), and self-rated well-being improved (p < 0.05) [17].

Now the other half. The authors called it a pilot. Text tests and a tapping test showed no significant difference, so not every measure moved. The authors said the dose was probably sub-optimal. The researchers were linked to the extract's manufacturer, and the study is 26 years old [17].

Beyond that single study, a 2012 systematic review found the evidence on Rhodiola and fatigue contradictory, with all included studies at high or unclear risk of bias [18]. A 2011 review concluded it may have beneficial effects but noted a lack of independent replication [19]. The EU hasn't authorised a Rhodiola health claim, and a claim about reducing tiredness in stress was refused in 2013 [25].

On our own product page we describe Rhodiola rosea as reducing mental and physical fatigue. That's our wording about our product. The research behind it is mixed, the studies used different extract doses from the 75 mg in a pouch, and you should weigh it that way. See our page on Rhodiola rosea.

Alpha-GPC: a small dose, and we'll say so

Alpha-GPC is a source of choline, which the body uses to make acetylcholine. The human studies in healthy adults used far more than one pouch has. A 2024 crossover trial in 20 men found a single 315 mg or 630 mg dose improved scores on one attention test (Stroop) but not on two others [16]. In 48 men, 250 mg a day for a week improved jump power versus placebo, while strength and reaction measures didn't differ [26]. A pouch has 100 mg, and two pouches give 200 mg.

In May 2026, EFSA's panel concluded L-alpha-GPC at up to 203.7 mg a day in food supplements raised no safety concerns under the applicant's proposed conditions [27]. That's a safety opinion on an ingredient. It doesn't approve NeuroPouch, and it says nothing about studying better. Treat Alpha-GPC as a supporting ingredient at a modest dose. More on the Alpha-GPC page.

Vitamin B12: the one with authorised wording

The wording the EU has authorised is that vitamin B12 contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue, and contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system [20]. Each pouch has 100 µg of methylcobalamin. That wording is about B12, not about the pouch as a whole. Details are on our vitamin B12 page.

Put together. Caffeine is there for alertness. L-theanine sits beside it for smoother attention switching, which is what moving between notes, past papers and an essay asks of you. Alpha-GPC is a choline source at a modest dose. Rhodiola is there for stress-related fatigue, with mixed evidence, and B12 carries its labelled wording. We chose the five as a set for steady focus under fatigue. That is a formulation choice, not a trial result.

Three rules sit behind that table.

The 400 mg ceiling counts everything. With two pouches at 200 mg you have 200 mg of daily headroom for coffee and tea. One average 16 oz café coffee is 188 mg [11], so two pouches plus that coffee puts you at 388 mg. If you use pouches, replace a coffee. Don't add to it.

The single-dose reference is 200 mg. Taking two pouches at once gives 200 mg, equal to EFSA's single-dose reference. The plan above spaces them nearly five hours apart on purpose.

The cut-off protects your sleep. EFSA notes that even 100 mg may affect sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime [13]. In one at-home study, 400 mg taken even six hours before bed reduced total sleep time [28], though that's four pouches' worth. A systematic review found caffeine usually lengthens the time to fall asleep, with large differences between people [29]. Caffeine's half-life ranged from 2.7 to 9.9 hours in one small study [30]. A 14:00 last pouch before an 11 pm bedtime leaves nine hours. We chose that gap as a cautious rule of thumb. It is not a studied threshold, and if you're a slow metaboliser you may want an earlier cut-off.

If a day doesn't need two, use one. Plenty of days don't.

How much caffeine is too much, and will it stop me sleeping?

"Safe" is not a word we use as a blanket claim, so here's what we can say. EFSA considers single doses of caffeine up to 200 mg and habitual intakes up to 400 mg a day from all sources unlikely to raise safety concerns for healthy adults [13]. Up to two pouches a day gives 200 mg from NeuroPouch. How you feel depends on your own body. Caffeine metabolism varies with genes, medicines and more [33], and a 2017 review concluded caffeine is relatively safe for healthy adults but can cause problems in vulnerable groups, including people with heart conditions [34].

NeuroPouch isn't for under-18s, pregnant or breastfeeding people, or anyone sensitive to caffeine. If you have a heart condition or take regular medication, ask a pharmacist or doctor first.

On sleep, caffeine can affect it, as covered above. That's why the plan has a cut-off, and why the second pouch is optional. If you find a pouch keeps you up, move it earlier or drop it.

Frequently asked questions

Is NeuroPouch for under-18s?

No. NeuroPouch is for adults aged 18 and over, which means university students, not school pupils. It is also not for pregnant or breastfeeding people, or anyone sensitive to caffeine. If you take medication or have a heart condition, ask a pharmacist or doctor before using any caffeine product.

How many pouches can I use on a revision day?

Up to two a day is the recommendation. Hold one under your upper lip for 20 to 40 minutes, then bin it. Each pouch has 100 mg of caffeine, and EFSA's reference for healthy adults is up to 400 mg a day from all sources and 200 mg in a single dose [13]. Keep the pouches hours apart.

Can I use NeuroPouch and still drink coffee?

Yes, but count everything. Two pouches give 200 mg, which leaves 200 mg of EFSA's 400 mg daily figure for coffee and tea [13]. One US 16 oz brewed coffee averaged 188 mg in a lab analysis [11], so on a two-pouch day one coffee is about your limit. A pouch in place of a coffee is simpler.

Will it help me get better grades?

We can't promise that, and no study has tested it. Studies on the caffeine and L-theanine pairing found better attention switching in healthy young adults in the lab, not better exam results [14]. Sleep, preparation and good teaching matter far more than any supplement.

Will it keep me awake at night?

It can if you use it late. EFSA notes that 100 mg of caffeine may affect sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime [13]. Use your last pouch early in the afternoon, as our plan does, which leaves nine hours before an 11 pm bedtime. If your sleep gets worse, move it earlier or drop it.
