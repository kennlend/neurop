A Focus Supplement for Traders That Doesn't Promise Better Trades

By NeuroPouch Team Updated November 2026 ~15 min read
Quick answer

A focus supplement for traders should help you stay alert across a long screen session without piling on caffeine or wrecking your sleep. NeuroPouch Energy & Focus is a nicotine-free pouch with 100 mg caffeine, 150 mg L-theanine, 100 mg Alpha-GPC, 75 mg Rhodiola rosea and 100 µg vitamin B12. It supports alertness. It does not make trades better, and nothing here promises returns.

It's 13:20 and the morning's volatility has burned off. The screens are the same, the charts are the same, and your attention has gone soft at the edges. You re-read the same headline twice. A level you marked at 08:15 has been hit and you didn't see it happen.

Or you're trading the US open from London. The session starts at 14:30 and runs on to 21:00, and by 19:00 you are making small, sloppy mistakes you wouldn't make at ten in the morning.

Nobody sells you a cure for that, because there isn't one. There are things that help: sleep, breaks, a rules-based plan, and for many people a measured dose of caffeine. This page explains what the research says about vigilance and caffeine, where a NeuroPouch fits, where it doesn't, and how to use it across a trading day without damaging your sleep.

One rule up front. Focus supports your process. It doesn't replace it, and it doesn't touch your edge. If a supplement page ever promises better trades, close the tab.

Why does your attention fade on a long screen session?

Watching for something to change is hard work. A review of vigilance research found that vigilance tasks are not undemanding: workload is high, performance tends to drop over time, and the tasks reduce engagement and increase distress, more so as they get harder [1]. That time-on-task decline is exactly what a trading screen asks of you for hours.

You can see it in the lab. In a two-hour sustained-attention task, error rates rose over time on placebo [2]. Nothing exotic happened to those volunteers. They just kept watching.

I won't link that to trading outcomes, because no study does. What it tells you is that the dip you feel at 13:20 is ordinary biology and not a character flaw. The body clock plays a part too. A review describes a mid-afternoon dip in performance that is partly a body-clock effect, occurs even if you skip lunch and can be worse after a high-carbohydrate lunch. It doesn't hit everyone equally [3].

Why don't coffee, energy drinks and pills fully fix it?

Coffee is the default, and for many traders it's fine. EFSA quotes about 90 mg for 200 ml of filter coffee and about 80 mg for a 60 ml espresso [4]. But cups vary. In an analysis of 97 shop espressos, caffeine ranged from 25 mg to 214 mg [5]. If your desk has four espressos before noon, at EFSA's typical 80 mg each, you may be near 320 mg by lunchtime without knowing it.

Regular drinkers also meet a quieter problem. In a study of 369 caffeine consumers, much of caffeine's apparent boost in regular users was the reversal of withdrawal [6]. Regular users can feel tired and foggy within a day of stopping, and symptoms have been reported after stopping doses as low as 100 mg a day [7]. Your third coffee may be keeping you level, not lifting you.

Energy drinks are a fixed can. A 250 ml can of Red Bull has 80 mg of caffeine and 27 g of sugar, according to the manufacturer's UK page [8]. They work in the short term, but you are drinking 27 g of sugar at your desk to get 80 mg.

Many stimulant pills and powders put a large dose in one hit, which is the opposite of what a long, steady session wants.

What's missing is a small, repeatable dose you can time, with something alongside the caffeine that was studied for attention. That's the case for a pouch.

What does the research say about caffeine and attention?

Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors. Doses from about 40 mg to 300 mg improve alertness, vigilance, attention and reaction time. And here is the line I won't skip: effects on memory and on higher-order functions, such as judgement and decision-making, are less consistent [9].

Read that twice if you trade. The best-supported thing caffeine does is keep you awake and watching. The research does not say it improves your decisions, and I'm not going to say it either.

In a two-hour vigilance task, error rates rose over time on placebo but not after 50 mg caffeine or 100 mg L-theanine. The combination was no better than either alone at those doses [2]. So even the pairing isn't always additive, which I'd rather you heard from me.

What about caffeine plus L-theanine for switching between things?

Trading is constant switching: chart, order book, news feed, chat, back to chart. The pairing studies look at exactly that kind of task.

  • In 27 healthy volunteers, 50 mg caffeine plus 100 mg L-theanine improved the speed and accuracy of switching between tasks compared with placebo [10].
  • A combination of 97 mg L-theanine and 40 mg caffeine improved accuracy when switching and raised self-reported alertness in 44 young adults, while other tasks showed no difference [11]. A repeat at the same doses again improved switching, although self-rated alertness did not change [12].
  • In a placebo-controlled crossover study, 150 mg caffeine combined with 250 mg L-theanine improved reaction times and accuracy on several attention tasks and lowered self-reported tiredness [13].
  • A 2014 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials found moderate benefits on alertness and attention switching within two hours of a dose. The caffeine dose appeared to matter more than the theanine dose [14].

The limits are real. These were acute, single-dose lab studies in healthy young adults, several funded by a tea company, and none tested 150 mg theanine with 100 mg caffeine, which is our 1.5 to 1 ratio. It sits inside the range the trials used, but nobody has run that exact test. There is a longer explanation on our caffeine and L-theanine page.

Will it make me jumpy on a red day?

Here the evidence is more complicated than a slogan, so I'll give you the numbers.

At a high dose of 250 mg caffeine, L-theanine did not significantly reduce jitteriness in a Bristol study of 48 adults, although it blunted caffeine's rise in blood pressure [15]. That dose is two and a half pouches in one go. A later study found non-regular caffeine consumers reported more jitteriness, and that caffeine did improve reaction speed and tapping in both groups [6].

Our product page says NeuroPouch gives smooth, jitter-free focus with no crash, and that is how Oliver, our founder, and more than 100 pre-launch testers described it. It's our description of the product, not a finding from those trials. No study has tested NeuroPouch.

The practical reading is simple. In these studies jitteriness showed up at higher doses and in people who don't drink caffeine regularly. A pouch is 100 mg, the same ballpark as a cup of filter coffee. On a volatile day the risk is stacking: three espressos, then a pouch, then a can. Count your milligrams on a red day more carefully than on a quiet one, not less.

How do the five ingredients work together in a focus supplement for traders?

Each pouch has 100 mg caffeine, 150 mg L-theanine, 100 mg Alpha-GPC, 75 mg Rhodiola rosea and 100 µg vitamin B12 (as methylcobalamin). Everything below describes research on ingredients, because no trial has tested the finished pouch.

Caffeine and L-theanine carry the attention job, as above. L-theanine has some separate research on calm. Resting brain recordings showed more alpha activity after 50 mg L-theanine than placebo, a pattern linked to relaxed alertness [16]. In 12 volunteers doing a stressful maths task, L-theanine lowered heart rate and a stress-related immune marker compared with placebo [17]. Both are small studies, neither included caffeine, and neither involved a trading screen. I'd call them a reason the pairing is interesting, not a promise about your nerves on a red day.

Alpha-GPC (100 mg) is a source of choline, which the body uses to make acetylcholine. See our Alpha-GPC page. A 2024 crossover trial in 20 men found a single 315 mg or 630 mg dose improved scores on one attention test but not on two others [18]. That is three to six times the dose in a pouch, so I won't pretend one pouch matches it. EFSA's panel concluded in May 2026 that L-alpha-GPC at up to 203.7 mg a day in food supplements raised no safety concerns under the applicant's proposed conditions [19]. That isn't an approval of NeuroPouch, and I haven't confirmed the Commission has authorised the ingredient. Two pouches contain 200 mg.

Rhodiola rosea (75 mg). Our product page says Rhodiola rosea reduces mental and physical fatigue. The research under that is mixed, so here it is straight. In 56 young physicians on night duty, a standardised Rhodiola extract improved scores on a set of mental performance tests compared with placebo [20]. The trial was by manufacturer-linked authors, and the physicians were healthy. A 2012 systematic review found the evidence contradictory, with studies at high or unclear risk of bias [21], and a 2011 review concluded Rhodiola may have beneficial effects but noted a lack of independent replication [22]. Rhodiola carries no authorised EU health claim, and a claim about reducing tiredness in stress was refused in 2013 [23]. Our Rhodiola page has more.

Vitamin B12 (100 µg). Vitamin B12 contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue, and to normal functioning of the nervous system. Those are the EU-authorised wordings, and they apply to the vitamin, not to the pouch as a whole [24]. More on the vitamin B12 page.

We picked the five as a set: caffeine for alertness, L-theanine beside it for smoother attention switching, Alpha-GPC as a source of choline, Rhodiola for stress-related fatigue (with the mixed evidence above) and B12 for its labelled wording. That is a formulation choice, not a trial result.

What about late sessions and short nights?

Trading calendars punish sleep. Pre-market prep starts early, and a US-hours session ends late.

A review of caffeine and occupational performance concluded that in jobs with restricted sleep, repeated dosing is an effective way to maintain capability, and repeated means spaced doses, not high totals [9]. In Navy SEAL trainees after three days without sleep, 200 to 300 mg of caffeine improved vigilance and reaction time, and 100 mg was less effective in that setting [25]. That's an extreme case and nothing like a bad night. I include it to be fair about the dose-response: more caffeine helped more, and more caffeine also costs you sleep.

That's the trap. Caffeine usually lengthens the time to fall asleep and shortens sleep, with large individual differences [26]. EFSA notes that even 100 mg may affect sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime [4]. In one at-home study, 400 mg taken even six hours before bed reduced total sleep time [27]. That's four pouches' worth, so don't read the size across, but the direction applies. Half-life ranged from 2.7 to 9.9 hours across ten healthy men [28], so your cutoff isn't mine.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best supplement for focus when trading?

No supplement has been tested on traders, so there is no research-backed best. The strongest evidence is for caffeine, which improves alertness, vigilance and reaction time [9]. A pouch pairs it with L-theanine and three other actives. Sleep, breaks and a rules-based plan matter more, and no supplement improves trading results.

Does caffeine help with decision-making?

Not reliably. Reviews find caffeine improves alertness, vigilance and reaction time, while its effects on judgement and decision-making are less consistent [9]. NeuroPouch supports alertness and attention. We don't claim it improves trading decisions, and nothing on this page promises better trades or returns.

Does L-theanine with caffeine reduce jitters?

In one Bristol study at 250 mg caffeine, L-theanine did not significantly reduce jitteriness [15]. The attention studies used lower caffeine doses. NeuroPouch has 100 mg caffeine with 150 mg L-theanine, and our own description is smooth, jitter-free focus. That description comes from our founder and 100-plus testers, not from a trial.

When should I take it on a trading day?

Start about 30 to 40 minutes before you need to be sharp, for example just before the open, and leave about eight hours between your last pouch and bedtime. That gap is our rule of thumb, not a study result. EFSA notes that even 100 mg may affect sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime [4].

How many pouches a day is too many?

We recommend up to two a day. Two pouches are 200 mg of caffeine, and EFSA considers up to 400 mg a day from all sources, and single doses up to 200 mg, unlikely to raise safety concerns for healthy adults [4]. Count your coffee and energy drinks as well, and take the pouches hours apart.
