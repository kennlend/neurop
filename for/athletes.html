Focus Supplement for Athletes: Keep Your Head in the Last Twenty Minutes

By NeuroPouch Team Updated November 2026 ~18 min read
Quick answer

A focus supplement for athletes should help you hold attention steady when a session gets long, and it has to fit inside your caffeine budget. NeuroPouch Energy & Focus is a nicotine-free pouch with 100 mg caffeine, 150 mg L-theanine, 100 mg Alpha-GPC, 75 mg Rhodiola rosea and 100 µg vitamin B12. At 100 mg per pouch it is a cognition-first dose, not a high-dose pre-workout, and it carries no sport certification.

It's minute 72. Your side is a goal up, or down, and the pitch has gone quiet in your ears. You're still running. What has slipped is the thing you can't see on a heart-rate strap: the half-second where you read the pass, pick the line, or choose whether to throw the shot.

Or it's the fifth working set on a Thursday evening after a full day at a desk. The bar isn't heavier than last week. It just feels like it is, and your attention keeps drifting to the clock on the wall.

If you train or compete seriously, you know both moments. Below: what the research says about mental fatigue in sport, what 100 mg of caffeine can and can't do for an athlete, where Alpha-GPC and Rhodiola help and where the evidence is thin, how to time a pouch around training and competition, and where anti-doping fits. I'll tell you where the evidence is weaker than the marketing around it.

Why do decisions slip late in a session?

In a 2009 experiment, 16 participants cycled to exhaustion at 80 per cent of peak power after either 90 minutes of a demanding computer task or 90 minutes of watching neutral documentaries. After the mental task, time to exhaustion fell from 754 seconds to 640, about 15 per cent shorter. Heart rate and breathing responses were the same in both conditions. The effort simply felt harder [1].

A systematic review of 11 studies reached the same shape of answer. Mental fatigue generally cut endurance performance and raised perceived exertion, while maximal strength, power and anaerobic work were not affected [2]. In those studies a tired head didn't weaken the muscles. It made a long effort feel worse.

You'll know the feeling. Late in a match, the cost can show up as a slightly worse choice rather than slower legs. In a long gym session, the fourth exercise starts to feel like a negotiation. That's anecdote, and the studies above measured cycling and perceived effort.

One caveat. Those studies tested mental fatigue, not supplements, so they don't show that a pouch fixes it. They tell you what to protect: attention and perceived effort across the whole session.

Why don't coffee, pre-workout and energy drinks fully solve it?

They all contain caffeine, and caffeine is the best-supported tool here. The trouble is how each one delivers it.

Coffee varies more than people think. EFSA quotes about 90 mg for 200 ml of filter coffee and about 80 mg for a 60 ml espresso [3]. Real cups drift. In an analysis of 97 espressos bought from shops, caffeine ranged from 25 mg to 214 mg per cup [4]. You can't dose a competition around that.

Energy drinks bring sugar and a fixed can. A 250 ml can of Red Bull has 80 mg of caffeine and 27 g of sugar, according to the manufacturer's UK page [5]. In a simulator study after a short night, an 80 mg caffeine energy drink cut sleepy-driving incidents for about 90 minutes [6]. So they do work, for a while. Controlled studies of energy-drink ingredients found caffeine was the main active ingredient and sugar added little [7].

Many pre-workout powders are built for a different job. They're designed around muscle work and a big caffeine hit, and the dose is whatever the scoop makes it. If your problem is attention in the last twenty minutes, a large dose at the start is a poorly timed answer. More on stacking in the objections section.

So the gap is a small, repeatable dose of caffeine, paired with something that rounds off its edges, that you can take with you in a pocket and time to the session. That's what a pouch is for.

What does caffeine actually do for athletes?

The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand on caffeine and exercise reviewed the field in 2021. It states that caffeine acutely improves many aspects of exercise performance in many, but not all, studies. The benefits are small to moderate for muscular endurance, strength, sprinting, jumping and throwing, and aerobic endurance is the most consistent. Doses of 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight improve performance consistently, the minimum effective dose may be as low as 2 mg/kg, and very high doses (9 mg/kg) cause many side effects without added benefit. The position stand also says caffeine is ergogenic for cognitive function, including attention and vigilance, in most individuals [8]. Several authors declare supplement-company ties, and the society receives industry grants.

A separate review adds a distinction worth keeping. Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors, and doses from about 40 mg to 300 mg improve alertness, vigilance, attention and reaction time. Its effects on higher-order functions such as judgement and decision-making are less consistent [9]. Physical benefits appear mainly above about 200 mg, or 3 mg/kg [9].

Where does a 100 mg pouch sit?

For a 70 kg athlete, 3 mg/kg is about 210 mg. A single 100 mg pouch is about 1.4 mg/kg. That's below the range tested in most sport trials and below the point where the review above places most physical benefit.

EFSA considers single doses of caffeine up to 200 mg, and habitual intakes up to 400 mg a day from all sources, unlikely to raise safety concerns for healthy adults [3]. A 90 kg athlete aiming for the position stand's 3 mg/kg would need 270 mg in one go, which is above EFSA's single-dose figure. Two pouches, spaced out, reach the lower end of the sport-research range for lighter athletes. If you want a large single ergogenic dose for a race, a dedicated caffeine product is the cheaper and more direct route, and you should plan it with EFSA's figures in front of you.

How should you time it?

The position stand names about 60 minutes before exercise as the most common timing, and says gum may need less lead time [8]. Caffeine chewing gum is absorbed through the cheek lining faster than a capsule, though total absorption is similar [10][11].

I'll be plain: we have not found a published absorption study of caffeine pouches, so we can't give you a pouch peak time from research. Our own description is that most people feel it within about 10 minutes, and the pouch sits under the upper lip for 20 to 40 minutes.

So the practical rule is to test it. Put a pouch in 30 to 40 minutes before the session, in training, and note when you feel it. Do that three or four times before you ever try it before a competition. Never debut anything on match day.

Caffeine also clears unevenly. In one study of ten healthy men, plasma half-life ranged from 2.7 to 9.9 hours [12], which is why evening training needs care.

How do the five ingredients work together in a focus supplement for athletes?

Each pouch contains 100 mg caffeine, 150 mg L-theanine, 100 mg Alpha-GPC, 75 mg Rhodiola rosea and 100 µg vitamin B12 (as methylcobalamin). Here's what each is there for, with the evidence told as it is. No study has tested NeuroPouch itself, so everything below describes research on the ingredients.

Caffeine 100 mg and L-theanine 150 mg: the attention pair

This is the core of the formula. Our caffeine and L-theanine guide goes deeper.

In a placebo-controlled crossover study in healthy adults, 150 mg caffeine combined with 250 mg L-theanine improved reaction times and accuracy on several attention tasks and lowered self-reported tiredness. Caffeine alone also helped [13]. A 2014 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials found moderate benefits of the pairing on alertness and attention switching within two hours of a dose, and the caffeine dose appeared to matter more than the theanine dose [14].

Now the honest part. All of these were single-dose lab studies in healthy, mostly young adults, and some were funded by a tea company. None tested 150 mg theanine with 100 mg caffeine, which is our ratio of 1.5 to 1. It sits inside the range the trials used, but nobody has run that exact test. In a two-hour sustained-attention task, errors rose over time on placebo but not after 50 mg caffeine or 100 mg theanine, and the combination was no better than either alone at those doses [15].

On jitters, the evidence is more complicated than a slogan. At a high caffeine dose of 250 mg, L-theanine did not significantly reduce jitteriness in a Bristol study, although it blunted caffeine's rise in blood pressure [16]. A later study of 369 caffeine consumers found non-regular consumers reported more jitteriness, and that much of caffeine's apparent boost in regular users was the reversal of withdrawal [17].

What we say about our own pouch is that it gives smooth, jitter-free focus and no crash. That is how Oliver, the founder, and the 100-plus people who tried it before launch described it. It's our description of the product. It isn't a finding from those trials, and I'd rather you hear that from us.

For athletes the useful reading is modest. At 100 mg you are below the 250 mg dose that raised jitteriness in the Bristol study, and theanine plausibly softens the edges. Attention switching is a skill a midfielder or a fighter uses, though lab tasks are not a game.

Alpha-GPC 100 mg: a promising ingredient at a dose below the trials

Alpha-GPC is a source of choline, which the body uses to make acetylcholine. Our Alpha-GPC page has the background. The sport-related human studies are small, and the doses are higher than ours.

  • In 48 men, 250 mg a day for a week improved jump power versus placebo, but strength and reaction measures did not differ [18].
  • A 2024 crossover trial in 20 resistance-trained men found a single 315 mg or 630 mg dose improved scores on one attention test (Stroop) but not on two others [19]. The ingredient was a branded one from a supplier.

So the human trials used 250 to 630 mg, and a pouch has 100 mg (two pouches, 200 mg). I'm not going to tell you one pouch will do what 600 mg did in a trial. This is a research area with signals worth watching, and attention is our target rather than your one-rep max.

On regulation: EFSA's panel concluded in May 2026 that L-alpha-GPC at up to 203.7 mg a day in food supplements raised no safety concerns under the applicant's proposed conditions [20]. That is a safety opinion on an applicant's ingredient. It doesn't approve NeuroPouch, and I haven't verified that the European Commission has authorised the ingredient.

Rhodiola rosea 75 mg: mixed evidence, told straight

Our product page says Rhodiola rosea reduces mental and physical fatigue. Here is what the research under that sits on. The Rhodiola page has the full picture.

In 24 healthy volunteers, a single 200 mg dose of Rhodiola extract lengthened time to exhaustion by about 2 per cent, but four weeks of daily use did not change the results [21]. In 18 people, a single dose of 3 mg/kg cut a 6-mile cycling time trial by about 0.4 minutes and lowered perceived effort [22]. A 2023 review of 13 trials found acute doses tended to improve endurance and lower perceived effort, but most studies were at unclear or high risk of bias [23]. A 2012 systematic review called the evidence on fatigue contradictory [24], while a 2011 review concluded Rhodiola may have beneficial effects but noted a lack of independent replication [25].

Three things follow. The sport effects are small, most trials are small and many involve the extract's manufacturer, and 3 mg/kg is about 210 mg for a 70 kg person, roughly three times the 75 mg in a pouch.

Rhodiola also carries no authorised EU health claim, and a claim about reducing tiredness in stress was refused in 2013 [26]. The studies are published research, not an EU-approved effect.

Vitamin B12 100 µg: the label-level claim

Vitamin B12 contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue, and it contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system. Those are the EU-authorised wordings, and they apply to the vitamin, not to the pouch as a whole [27]. There's more on the vitamin B12 page. It's the quiet member of the formula, and I wouldn't ask it to do more than that sentence says.

Put together. Caffeine is the alertness ingredient. L-theanine sits beside it because the pairing studies found better attention switching, which is the skill a late session asks for. Alpha-GPC adds a choline source at a modest dose. Rhodiola's best-known research is on stress-related fatigue in non-athletes: in 56 young physicians on night duty, a standardised extract improved scores on mental performance tests compared with placebo, in a trial by manufacturer-linked authors [28]. Its sport results are small. B12 carries its labelled wording. We chose the five as a set for steady focus under fatigue. That is a formulation decision, not a trial result.

What does a training-day and competition-day plan look like?

The sums come first. Two pouches is 200 mg of caffeine, which equals EFSA's single-dose figure and half its daily figure [3]. That leaves 200 mg for coffee and tea. EFSA also notes that even 100 mg may affect sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime [3].

In one at-home study, 400 mg taken even six hours before bed reduced total sleep time [30]. That dose is four pouches' worth, so don't read its size across, but the principle holds. So protect your sleep.

"I already take pre-workout."

Count before you add anything. A pouch adds 100 mg, and the label on your tub decides the rest. "Can I use a pouch during training?"

Use it before. A pouch sits under the upper lip for 20 to 40 minutes, then goes in the bin. Put one in as you warm up, and take it out before anything involving a mouthguard, contact or hard mouth-breathing.

"Is it safe, and how much caffeine is too much?"

We don't use "safe" as a blanket word. EFSA considers single doses up to 200 mg, and habitual intakes up to 400 mg a day from all sources, unlikely to raise safety concerns for healthy adults [3]. Two pouches are 200 mg, spread through the day. A 2017 review found caffeine can cause problems in vulnerable groups, including people with heart conditions [31]. NeuroPouch isn't for under-18s, pregnant or breastfeeding people, or anyone sensitive to caffeine. If you take medication or have a medical condition, ask your doctor first.

"Will it wreck my sleep?"

It can if you use it late, which is why the plan above has cut-offs. EFSA notes that even 100 mg may affect sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime [3]. Count back at least six hours from bed, and drop the second pouch if your sleep is already short.

Frequently asked questions

Is caffeine banned in sport?

No. WADA does not list caffeine as a prohibited substance. It sits on WADA's Monitoring Program, which collects data on how it is used and does not make it prohibited [29]. The list changes every year, so check the current version and your sport's rules, and remember that every other ingredient in a supplement needs checking too.

How much caffeine do athletes need?

The ISSN position stand reports consistent benefits at 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight and says the minimum effective dose may be as low as 2 mg/kg [8]. One NeuroPouch has 100 mg, about 1.4 mg/kg for a 70 kg athlete, and two pouches reach about 2.9 mg/kg. EFSA's single-dose figure for healthy adults is 200 mg [3].

Does a focus supplement help with mental fatigue in sport?

Mental fatigue lowers endurance performance and makes the same effort feel harder [1][2]. Caffeine improves alertness, vigilance and reaction time [9]. No study has tested a caffeine pouch against mental fatigue in athletes, so the link is indirect, and NeuroPouch itself has not been trialled.

Can I take NeuroPouch with a pre-workout or energy drink?

Add up the caffeine first. EFSA considers single doses up to 200 mg, and up to 400 mg a day from all sources, unlikely to raise safety concerns for healthy adults [3]. One pouch plus a pre-workout labelled 200 mg is 300 mg in one sitting. Most athletes are better off choosing one caffeine source per session.

How long before training should I use a pouch?

Our suggestion is 30 to 40 minutes before, tested in training first. The ISSN names about 60 minutes before exercise as the usual timing for caffeine in general [8]. We have not found a published absorption study of caffeine pouches, so we can't give you a research-based peak time.

Is NeuroPouch certified for sport?

No. NeuroPouch is a food supplement and carries no third-party anti-doping certification, and we make no sport-certification claim. If you are tested, check every ingredient against the current WADA list and your sport's rules, and speak to your governing body before using anything new.
