What is a caffeine pouch, and how fast does it work?

A caffeine pouch is a small porous sachet filled with caffeine and, in some products, other ingredients. You tuck it between your upper lip and gum, leave it for a while, then bin it. There's no tobacco and no nicotine, and you don't chew or swallow it. The format borrows its look from nicotine pouches, which is part of why regulators and campaigners have started paying attention.

How fast caffeine reaches your blood from a pouch is an open question. We found no published absorption study of caffeine pouches. The nearest relative is chewing gum. In an 84-man study, caffeine gum reached peak blood levels in 44 to 80 minutes, against 84 to 120 minutes for the same doses in a capsule, while total absorption was similar [2]. A review of alternative caffeine formats reached the same conclusion: faster through the cheek lining for gum, no difference in the total absorbed [3].

Swallowed caffeine is quick anyway. In a small 1983 study of ten men, an oral dose peaked in the blood in roughly half an hour and was almost completely absorbed [4]. So the case for a pouch rests on convenience and a measured dose, and a speed advantage is unproven. NeuroPouch's own description is that most people feel it within about 10 minutes, which matches what our founder reports. Nobody has measured it in a trial.

Are caffeine pouches legal in Europe and the UK?

They're on sale, mostly as food supplements. Whether anyone has written rules specifically for them is a different matter.

As of early 2026, trade press describes a regulatory grey area. NutraIngredients reported in February 2026 that Denmark's food authority had begun assessing caffeine sachets, while most of Europe has no pouch-specific rules, and a food-law specialist quoted in the piece called the position fragmented [5]. A French anti-smoking organisation made a similar point in 2025, describing the segment as lacking specific regulation [6]. We couldn't trace the Danish steps to a Danish government document, so treat that detail with caution. We also found no statement on caffeine pouches from the European Commission, EFSA, Germany's BfR or the UK Food Standards Agency.

Three things we can say with more confidence:

  • EFSA's caffeine opinion covers caffeine in any food, whatever the format [1].
  • In the UK, the Food Standards Agency and Food Standards Scotland published guidance on caffeine in food supplements in September 2024, after a death linked to caffeine powder [7]. It isn't pouch-specific and we haven't confirmed whether it covers pouches. We're not stating a UK legal cap because we couldn't verify one.
  • Age checks vary. The Grocer reported in January 2026 that UK caffeine pouches have no age restriction comparable to the planned limits on high-caffeine energy drinks, although specialist online retailers refuse sales to under-18s [8].

For a brand, that's a gap. For a buyer, the label and the seller's behaviour become your main protection. Check that the dose is printed per pouch, that the seller states an age limit, and that the product says who shouldn't use it. NeuroPouch is not for under-18s, pregnant or breastfeeding people, or anyone sensitive to caffeine.

How much caffeine is too much? The EFSA numbers

EFSA is the European Union's food-safety authority, and its 2015 opinion is the main reference for caffeine limits in Europe. For healthy adults it considers single doses up to 200 mg (about 3 mg per kg of body weight) and habitual intakes up to 400 mg a day (about 5.7 mg/kg) as not raising safety concerns, counting all sources. For pregnant and breastfeeding women the figure is up to 200 mg a day [1].

EFSA also notes that 100 mg (about 1.4 mg/kg) may affect sleep in some adults, especially close to bedtime [1]. That is the most useful line in the opinion for anyone tempted by a pouch at 4 pm.

The wording is "do not raise safety concerns". It isn't a certificate for every person, and it assumes a healthy adult. People with heart conditions, those on certain medicines and anyone sensitive to caffeine should ask a doctor first. A 2017 review concluded that caffeine is relatively safe for healthy adults but can cause problems in vulnerable groups, including people with heart conditions [9].

One pouch is about the caffeine in a cup of filter coffee or a strong espresso. And coffee varies where a pouch doesn't: in the US study, the same drink from the same shop ranged from 259 mg to 564 mg across six days [12]. A printed dose is easier to plan around than a barista's mood.

A worked day

Say you drink one filter coffee at 08:00 (about 90 mg [1]) and take one pouch at 13:30 (100 mg). That's about 190 mg, inside both the 200 mg single-dose figure and the 400 mg daily figure. A second pouch later brings the day to about 290 mg. Spacing matters, which is why NeuroPouch recommends up to two pouches a day rather than two at once. Two pouches is 200 mg, half of EFSA's daily figure, which leaves 200 mg of headroom for coffee and tea.

Sleep is the cost to watch. In one at-home study, 400 mg of caffeine taken even six hours before bed reduced total sleep time [13]. That's four pouches in one go, so don't read the size of the effect across to a single pouch. A systematic review found caffeine typically lengthens the time it takes to fall asleep and shortens sleep, with large differences between people [14], and half-lives in the 1983 study ranged from about 3 to 10 hours [4]. Pick a cut-off hour that suits you and test it for a week.

Do the extra ingredients in focus pouches matter?

Some have research behind them. None has been tested in the finished pouch, and no trial exists for NeuroPouch itself. Keep that in mind whichever brand you buy.

Caffeine with L-theanine has the most evidence. A 2014 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials found moderate benefits of the pairing on alertness and attention switching in the first two hours after a dose, and noted that the caffeine dose appeared to matter more than the theanine dose [22]. The caveats are real. The trials were acute, single doses in healthy young adults, and several came from Unilever researchers. No trial tested 150 mg theanine with 100 mg caffeine. The 1.5:1 ratio sits inside the range that was tested, about 0.8:1 to 2.4:1.

On jitters, the evidence is mixed. At a high caffeine dose (250 mg), L-theanine did not significantly reduce jitteriness in one Bristol study, though it blunted caffeine's rise in blood pressure [23]. A later study of 369 caffeine consumers found non-regular users reported more jitteriness, and that tolerance to it builds with habitual use [24]. NeuroPouch describes its effect as smooth, jitter-free focus. That's our description, based on the dose and on what more than 100 pre-launch testers told us, and it isn't a study result.

Alpha-GPC is a source of choline, which the body uses to make acetylcholine. The human performance and cognition trials used 250 to 630 mg a day [25][26]. A pouch has 100 mg and two pouches give 200 mg, so we're below the doses that were tested. In May 2026 EFSA's panel concluded that L-alpha-GPC at up to 203.7 mg a day in food supplements raised no safety concerns under the applicant's proposed conditions [27]. That is a safety opinion. It isn't an authorisation and doesn't approve any product, so we don't call alpha-GPC "EU-authorised".

Rhodiola rosea has small trials, mostly linked to the manufacturer of one extract. A 2012 systematic review called the evidence contradictory, with every included study at high or unclear risk of bias [28]. The study doses vary and aren't equivalent to a 75 mg pouch. The EU has no authorised health claim for Rhodiola: a claim about reducing tiredness in stress was refused in 2013 [29]. NeuroPouch's description is that Rhodiola reduces mental and physical fatigue. That's our product copy, and it isn't an EU-endorsed effect.

Vitamin B12 is different, because the EU has approved wording: vitamin B12 contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue, and to normal functioning of the nervous system [30]. The claim belongs to B12, not to the pouch as a whole.

Caffeine has no EU-authorised alertness claim that we could find in the claims regulation [30], so we describe studies and not claims. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, and doses from about 40 mg to 300 mg improve alertness, vigilance and reaction time, with less consistent effects on judgement and decision-making [31].

Use this list on any brand, ours included.

  1. Caffeine per pouch, printed on the label. "Contains caffeine" isn't enough. You need milligrams.
  2. Other ingredients, with doses. If a brand names actives without amounts, you can't tell a meaningful dose from a sprinkle.

Who should skip caffeine pouches?

Anyone under 18. Anyone pregnant or breastfeeding (EFSA's figure is 200 mg a day from all sources, so ask a professional about any extra) [1]. Anyone sensitive to caffeine, with a heart condition, or on medicines that interact with it. If 100 mg makes you anxious or wrecks your sleep, a pouch isn't for you, and decaf plus a walk is a fine answer.

If you drink caffeine daily, stopping can leave you tired and foggy within a day. That's withdrawal, and symptoms have been reported after stopping doses as low as 100 mg a day [32]. Part of the lift regular users feel is the reversal of that [24]. It's a good reason to decide on a dose and stick to it, instead of stacking pouches on coffee on an energy drink.

Coffee is the better choice if you enjoy the ritual or don't need a measured dose. A pouch fits when you want a fixed amount and can't sit with a drink, or when the shop cup could be anywhere from 25 mg to 214 mg [11].

Frequently asked questions

Are caffeine pouches legal in the UK and Europe?

They're on sale, mainly as food supplements. Trade press reported in February 2026 that Denmark's food authority had begun assessing caffeine sachets, while most of Europe has no pouch-specific rules [5]. We found no official statement on caffeine pouches from the European Commission, EFSA or the UK FSA. Check the seller's age limit and the printed caffeine dose before you buy.

How much caffeine is in a caffeine pouch?

It varies by brand. Products we checked range from 50 mg (LEWA Power, XQS) to 80 mg (Cafero) to 100 mg (NeuroPouch, Hicaff) and 180 mg (Ultra Energy, which doesn't ship to Europe) [15][17]. For healthy adults, EFSA considers single doses up to 200 mg and daily intakes up to 400 mg from all sources as raising no safety concern [1].

Are caffeine pouches better than energy drinks or coffee?

Different, not automatically better. A pouch gives a printed dose with no sugar. A can of Red Bull has 80 mg of caffeine and 27 g of sugar [10], and a shop espresso can range from 25 to 214 mg [11]. No study has compared pouches with energy drinks or coffee, so a claim that one beats the other is a judgement, not a research result.

Do caffeine pouches work faster than coffee?

It's unproven. We found no published absorption study of caffeine pouches. Research on caffeine gum shows faster peak blood levels than capsules, with similar total absorption [2][3]. NeuroPouch's own description is that most people feel it within about 10 minutes.

Can I buy US caffeine pouch brands in Europe or the UK?

Often not. Ultra says it ships to the US and Canada, Nectr to the US only, and Roon describes US shipping without mentioning the EU or UK [17][19][20]. "Not stated" isn't a "no", but check each brand's shipping page and the checkout total, including VAT and customs, before you order.

Who shouldn't use caffeine pouches?

Under-18s, pregnant or breastfeeding people, and anyone sensitive to caffeine should avoid them. Check with a doctor first if you have a heart condition or take medicines. EFSA notes that even 100 mg can affect sleep in some adults if taken close to bedtime [1].
